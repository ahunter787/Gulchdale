# Gulchdale Design Charter

## 1. Identity

**Gulchdale is a draftable multiplayer Magic experience with Commander roots, but it is not bound by the rules or assumptions of Commander Cube.**

The purpose of Gulchdale is to create memorable multiplayer games in which players feel that their decks were **forged through choices, discoveries, preferences, and circumstances**, rather than assembled from a sequence of unrelated random packs.

The draft itself is part of the game experience.

Gulchdale should feel like a place players repeatedly return to, explore, and gradually become familiar with.

### Core Principle

**A Gulchdale deck should feel forged, not assembled by accident.**

---

# 2. Concepts Retained from Commander and Cube

## Singleton Deck Construction

Traditional Commander Cube is generally singleton across the entire cube.

Gulchdale instead uses **singleton within the final drafted deck**.

The larger Gulchdale card pool may contain multiple copies of cards when doing so supports multiple players or archetypes.

For example, Gulchdale should be capable of supporting two Goblin players in the same draft. Both players may receive access to a common foundation of Goblin cards without either player consuming the entire archetype.

Duplicates are therefore a **draft-system resource**, not necessarily a deck-building permission.

Unless a specific rule or Adornment says otherwise, a completed Gulchdale deck contains no duplicate non-basic cards.

## Commander as an Anchor

Commanders remain important.

They provide:

- mechanical direction;
- personality;
- color identity;
- thematic identity;
- archetype signals;
- a recognizable centerpiece for a deck.

However, the commander does not have absolute authority over every aspect of deck construction.

The commander begins the player's journey. Gulchdale can change where that journey leads.

---

# 3. Concepts Gulchdale Deliberately Abandons

## Absolute Commander Color Identity

Commander color identity is a starting constraint rather than an immutable law.

Draft Effects, Adornments, Expeditions, discoveries, or other Gulchdale mechanics may:

- add a color;
- permit particular off-color cards;
- unlock a faction or tribe;
- modify deck-construction rules;
- modify a commander;
- grant access to cards that would normally violate Commander identity.

These exceptions should be visible and understandable to the player.

They should feel like something the player **earned, discovered, or chose**, rather than an arbitrary rules exception.

## Cube-Wide Singleton

Gulchdale does not require the global card pool to be singleton.

The pool should contain enough archetype infrastructure for several related decks to coexist.

## Commander as the Definition of the Format

Gulchdale recognizes its Commander ancestry without requiring every future game mode to satisfy Commander conventions.

Future Gulchdale formats may emphasize:

- mono-color decks;
- two-color decks;
- tribal decks;
- commanders;
- non-commander leaders;
- alternate deck construction;
- expeditions with changing rules.

The quality of the Gulchdale experience takes priority over adherence to an inherited format.

---

# 4. Player Experience Goals

## Powerful Games Without Competitive-Magic Texture

Gulchdale decks should be powerful.

There should be very few cards that feel embarrassing to draw.

Players should regularly experience:

- strong value engines;
- complicated board states;
- powerful synergies;
- meaningful stack interaction;
- explosive turns;
- memorable creatures and spells.

However, Gulchdale should avoid becoming a format in which players are expected to independently hold answers to every threat.

The preferred multiplayer solution is often:

> "That is the threat. We need to solve it together."

Politics are therefore an intentional part of the game system.

## Politics-Supported Interaction

Interaction must exist in sufficient quantity for politics to matter.

A political agreement is meaningless if the players involved are incapable of acting on it.

At the same time, Gulchdale should avoid interaction density so high that important permanents cease to matter.

Particularly:

**It is not enjoyable to wait several turns to deploy a commander or centerpiece card only for it to be trivially removed every time it enters play.**

Threats should have enough durability, immediate value, protection opportunities, or political consequence that playing an important card feels meaningful.

## Complexity Without Exclusion

Players have generally responded positively to:

- complex engines;
- difficult stack interactions;
- unusual card interactions;
- high-value sequencing;
- elaborate board states.

The problem is frequently not that the complexity exists.

The problem is that less experienced players may not know how to operate it.

Gulchdale should therefore preserve sophisticated play while finding ways to **explain, surface, recommend, or narrate complexity**.

The goal is not to make every deck simple.

The goal is to help the appropriate player understand the deck they have built.

---

# 5. Threat and Interaction Philosophy

Gulchdale should maintain tension between three failures.

### Failure A — The Invincible Threat

A permanent dominates the game because the pool lacks realistic ways to interact with it.

This is undesirable.

### Failure B — The Disposable Threat

Important permanents cannot survive long enough to matter because efficient removal is overwhelmingly plentiful.

This is also undesirable.

### Failure C — The Self-Sufficient Player

One player consistently possesses enough interaction to independently answer every major threat.

This reduces politics and turns multiplayer Magic into several simultaneous one-on-one games.

This is undesirable.

### Desired State

Players should have meaningful interaction, but solving the largest threats should often require:

- cooperation;
- timing;
- negotiation;
- resource expenditure;
- multiple players contributing;
- or allowing another player's threat to temporarily remain because something worse exists.

---

# 6. Bomb Philosophy

Big cards should feel big.

When an expensive or dramatic card resolves, the table should remember it.

A bomb should preferably create:

- a dramatic board state;
- a new political problem;
- an unusual decision;
- an exciting opportunity;
- or a temporary shift in power.

A bomb should not generally create an overwhelming advantage before meaningful counterplay can begin.

For example, a card such as **Primeval Titan** can create too much permanent advantage simply by resolving and triggering once. Removing it afterward may technically answer the creature while failing to answer what the card actually accomplished.

Cards of this nature should receive particular scrutiny.

Gulchdale prefers:

**dramatic impact with continuing interaction**

over

**irreversible advantage disguised as a removable permanent.**

---

# 7. Pace

The intended pace should resemble slower multiplayer Commander from approximately the mid-2010s more than modern optimized Commander.

Players should have time to:

- establish an identity;
- deploy engines;
- understand opponents' strategies;
- negotiate;
- make mistakes and recover;
- build toward large turns.

Acceleration is allowed.

Explosive turns are allowed.

Powerful cards are encouraged.

But Gulchdale should resist environments where the game is functionally decided before players have had an opportunity to inhabit their decks.

---

# 8. Beginner Experience

Fun multiplayer Magic is difficult to produce consistently because different players achieve fun through different levels and forms of complexity.

Gulchdale should not solve this by flattening the entire environment.

Instead, it should help players find experiences appropriate to them.

## The Three-Color Trap

A beginner can draft a mechanically powerful three-color deck and still have a poor experience.

The deck may impose a cognitive and mana-management burden disproportionate to the player's familiarity with:

- the card pool;
- sequencing;
- mana fixing;
- mulligan decisions;
- color requirements;
- threat assessment;
- archetype interactions.

A veteran player can exploit this same complexity to great effect.

Therefore:

**Draft success and playability are not the same metric.**

## Player Level and Complexity

Cards, archetypes, commanders, and eventually generated decks should carry metadata describing relevant difficulty.

Possible dimensions include:

- color complexity;
- mechanical complexity;
- sequencing difficulty;
- rules complexity;
- synergy dependence;
- stack interaction;
- graveyard management;
- resource tracking;
- punishment for mistakes.

Player profiles may contain a corresponding experience or confidence level.

The draft system can then help newer players find decks that are:

**powerful but easier to pilot.**

This assistance should generally be subtle.

A beginner should still feel that they drafted their deck themselves.

---

# 9. Mono-Color, Tribal, and Accessible Archetypes

Strong player interest in mono-color tribal strategies should be treated as a meaningful design signal.

Gulchdale should deliberately support archetypes that provide:

- strong thematic identity;
- clear card-evaluation signals;
- consistent mana;
- recognizable synergies;
- satisfying mechanical progression.

Mono-color and primarily mono-color decks with small splashes may be particularly useful for newer players.

These archetypes should not necessarily be weaker than complex multicolor strategies.

Complexity is not a prerequisite for power.

---

# 10. Guided Drafting

Gulchdale may influence drafts through questions, Expeditions, discoveries, previous picks, commanders, and player history.

Example:

> You've found an abandoned settlement. What interests you most?

A player might choose:

- the Library;
- the Forge;
- the Graveyard;
- the Market;
- the Barracks.

These choices alter the probability of future cards appearing.

The mechanical consequences should usually remain beneath the surface.

The player experiences a world.

The system experiences weights.

## Soft Guidance

Guidance should affect probability rather than dictate outcomes.

Gulchdale should help players discover coherent decks without turning the draft into a predetermined deck generator.

The player must retain opportunities to:

- pivot;
- experiment;
- abandon an archetype;
- discover something unexpected;
- compete for cards;
- interpret ambiguous signals.

---

# 11. Personalization

Gulchdale should learn from repeated play.

Player history may include:

- cards drafted;
- cards passed;
- commanders considered;
- commanders chosen;
- archetypes drafted;
- colors preferred;
- questionnaire choices;
- final deck construction;
- cards repeatedly prioritized;
- player experience;
- match outcomes;
- recurring preferences.

The playgroup may be strongly archetyped.

This is not something Gulchdale needs to eliminate.

Players often enjoy repeatedly returning to strategies they love.

Gulchdale should make it natural for a player to drift toward familiar favorites while still periodically presenting attractive reasons to explore something new.

---

# 12. Pet Cards

Repeated attachment to particular cards is an important part of the Gulchdale experience.

Examples include:

- Deathrender;
- Shifting Wildwood;
- Pilgrim's Eye.

The system may learn that a player disproportionately values a particular card.

That information may gently increase the likelihood that the card enters that player's draft environment.

Pet-card influence must remain probabilistic.

A player's favorite card should sometimes seem to **find them**.

It should not simply be delivered to them.

The difference is important.

---

# 13. Fairness and Curation

Gulchdale is a heavily curated environment.

Raw card power is welcome.

Oppressive play patterns are not.

Certain cards may technically be balanced according to traditional metrics while still damaging the environment by:

- invalidating too many strategies;
- removing meaningful interaction;
- rewarding little investment;
- suppressing experimentation;
- creating repetitive games;
- creating disproportionate frustration;
- becoming automatic picks;
- reducing political interaction.

Examples requiring scrutiny include effects such as:

- difficult-to-interact-with indestructible threats;
- certain protection-heavy equipment such as Sword of X and Y effects;
- cards that create extreme resource advantage immediately;
- cards with unusually high frustration or salt ratings.

External resources such as EDHREC Game Changer classifications, salt data, play rates, and community feedback may inform investigation.

They are **signals, not automatic bans.**

Final decisions belong to Gulchdale's curator.

---

# 14. Data-Informed Design

Gulchdale should record enough information to understand how the game actually behaves.

Draft telemetry may include:

- cards offered;
- cards picked;
- cards passed;
- pick order;
- commander options;
- commander selections;
- Expedition and questionnaire choices;
- archetypes inferred;
- final deck construction;
- cards excluded from the final deck;
- player familiarity;
- game outcomes;
- recurring card and archetype preferences.

This information can be used to measure both **game health** and **experience health**.

Examples:

- Can two players successfully occupy the same tribe?
- Are particular colors under-drafted?
- Does an archetype exist in theory but fail to produce coherent decks?
- Are beginners disproportionately pushed toward difficult mana bases?
- Are certain cards automatic first picks?
- Does an archetype frequently produce cards that never make the final deck?
- Do particular commanders consistently lead players into unsuccessful drafts?
- Are players finding their favorite strategies without being trapped in them?

Telemetry informs curation.

It does not replace judgment.

---

# 15. Gulchdale's Source of Truth

The Gulchdale application should ultimately become the canonical source for:

- card pool;
- duplicate quantities;
- archetype metadata;
- environmental metadata;
- power assessments;
- complexity assessments;
- external data;
- human overrides;
- player data;
- draft history;
- rules;
- format definitions.

Cube Cobra remains valuable as a public representation, analysis tool, and distribution surface.

Draftmancer remains valuable as the foundation of the live drafting interface.

Neither should define Gulchdale's internal model.

---

# 16. Buildability and Portability

Gulchdale should eventually be reproducible by someone other than its creator.

The system should track:

- card availability;
- printings;
- replacement options;
- cost;
- expensive outliers;
- functional alternatives;
- required physical components.

A Gulchdale deployment should eventually be capable of supporting:

- a private playgroup;
- another individual building a copy;
- a game store deployment;
- a convention or event;
- a maintained community installation.

The current pool may contain expensive cards, but affordability should become an explicit design consideration rather than an afterthought.

---

# 17. Roles

One person currently performs all Gulchdale roles, with AI assistance wherever useful.

The roles should nevertheless remain conceptually separate so they can eventually be delegated.

## Creative / Product Director

Defines what Gulchdale is and protects the overall vision.

## Game Systems Designer

Defines draft structure, Expeditions, Adornments, rules, progression, player choices, and pack-generation behavior.

## Cube Curator

Maintains the card pool, tribes, archetypes, power environment, duplicates, additions, and removals.

## Data and Recommendation Designer

Maintains card metadata, archetype relationships, weighting algorithms, recommendation systems, external datasets, and player-affinity models.

## World / Experience Designer

Creates Gulchdale's locations, narrative framing, questions, terminology, art direction, and sense of place.

## Platform Engineer

Builds the Gulchdale application, backend services, databases, administration tools, authentication, APIs, and deployment systems.

## Draft Engine Engineer

Maintains Gulchdale's limited modifications to Draftmancer and keeps the integration surface as small as practical.

## Playtest and Analytics Lead

Designs playtests, examines draft and game telemetry, identifies problems, and evaluates proposed changes.

## Physical Product and Accessibility Lead

Tracks affordability, reproducibility, substitute cards, physical deployment, beginner usability, and game-store requirements.

## Release / QA Lead

Maintains tests, reproducible releases, migrations, compatibility, and stable deployed versions.

## Publishing / Legal Strategy

Maintains awareness of licensing, intellectual-property boundaries, third-party data terms, distribution restrictions, and possible future commercial relationships.

---

# 18. Decision Hierarchy

When two design goals conflict, Gulchdale should generally prioritize them in this order:

1. A memorable and enjoyable multiplayer experience.
2. Player agency.
3. Healthy political interaction.
4. Coherent and satisfying decks.
5. Accessibility without flattening complexity.
6. Replayability and discovery.
7. Balance.
8. Fidelity to Commander conventions.

Commander tradition is valuable.

It is not more important than Gulchdale.

---

# 19. Design Test

A proposed card, rule, mechanic, or software feature should ultimately answer:

**Does this help a player forge a memorable Gulchdale deck and enjoy the game that follows?**

If the answer is no, its inclusion should be questioned.
